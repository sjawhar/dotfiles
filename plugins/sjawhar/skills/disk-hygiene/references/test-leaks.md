# Leaks from tests and processes

Read this when the space or load comes from processes a test or harness left behind, or when you write such a test. `S` is as set in SKILL.md.

- Find the leak before you guess at it. `python3 $S cpu --seconds 5` groups CPU by command name, which is where a fan-out of one name hides. `sudo python3 $S writers --seconds 5` names the processes that write now, with their working directories. `python3 $S fdwrites --seconds 5 --pid <pid>` names the file one of them grows.
- A test that forks a detached server must reap it when the parent dies. A tool timeout is a SIGKILL that skips `finally`, so use a watchdog that polls the test's pid.
- Run each mutation-test mutant under a wall-clock `timeout`, in a process group that the harness kills on exit, with output capped (`ulimit -f`). A mutant without a guard can loop forever or fill the disk.
- A stopped process (`T` state) accepts `kill -TERM` with rc 0 and stays alive until `SIGCONT`. Read `ps -o stat=` before you trust a kill.
- After you kill a build, check for survivors by WHAT THEY RUN, not by what you killed: `pgrep -af <your work-dir path>`, then `ps -o pid,ppid,etime` on the hits, where a `PPID` of 1 and an elapsed time older than your kill is a reparented worker. A pool — loky, multiprocessing, `xargs -P`, `make -j` — outlives the process that started it whenever the parent dies without reaping, so the wrapper and the entry point go, the pool keeps running, and every check on the pids you killed comes back clean. Kill the process group (`kill -TERM -<pgid>`) and verify by path. One such survivor wrote 60.8 MB/s for seven minutes after its owner had honestly reported the build stopped, and suspending it returned 30.9 GB in forty seconds.
- Prefer `SIGSTOP` to a kill when the process is not yours: it stops the writing at once, loses nothing, and leaves its owner to resume or end it.
- A PID IS NOT A PROGRAM. `exec` keeps the pid and replaces what it runs, so a guard that records "pid, file descriptor, and what I saw there" and acts on it later acts on a stale identity with no error at any point: one such guard copied one script's bytes over another's path, and took the write bits of a file its record did not name, leaving it read-only with nothing to restore it. If you hold a reference to a running thing, re-verify the identity where you act — `stat /proc/<pid>/fd/<fd>` and compare dev and ino with what you recorded — not where you recorded it.
- A watcher that may kill is started with the exact pid of the one job it guards, kills only that pid and its process group, and exits when that pid exits. When you abandon an attempt, kill its watcher by the pid you recorded and read back that it is gone. Never stop or kill by a name pattern: on 2026-11-02 a disk watcher of an abandoned attempt outlived its kill and later killed a live build's wrapper.
- Decide that work has ended by the work itself: its process and its container are both gone and its log has an end line. Never by a wrapper's pid or exit code. The same night, a pid chain read a killed wrapper as "the build ended" and started the next build beside the one still running.
