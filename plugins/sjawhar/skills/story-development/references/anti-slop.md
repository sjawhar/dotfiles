# Structural anti-slop patterns

Cut for shape, pacing and rhythm across the whole piece, not for banned words. When you cut a tic, put back the concrete detail it displaced.

| # | Pattern | Tell |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Over-explanation | States a feeling or theme the scene already showed. Cut it first. It is usually most of the edit. |
| 2 | Reflexive groups of three | Three examples, adjectives or items by default, not because three is right here. |
| 3 | "Not X but Y" | The antithesis is a rhetorical shape, not the actual point. |
| 4 | Uniform paragraph length | Every paragraph four to six sentences. |
| 5 | Beats exactly on the outline | No beat arrives early, late or differently than planned. An outline is a plan, not a contract. |
| 6 | Same ending every time | Every scene ends on a cliffhanger, wry line or callback, regardless of what that particular scene needs. |
| 7 | Tension resolved early | The prose answers the question a scene raised instead of sitting in it. |
| 8 | Ambiguity collapsed | The prose resolves an ambiguity the author placed on purpose. |

## Prose line passes

- Triage flagged lines before you fix them: P0 credibility killers (a line that breaks belief in the scene), P1 obvious tells (a pattern listed above), P2 stylistic polish. Use this for prose only, not plot or structure.
- Tell the author which passages you drafted, so they can disclose model prose: experienced readers detect it reliably.
- Automated AI detectors are unreliable. Treat their output as a signal to investigate, not a verdict.
